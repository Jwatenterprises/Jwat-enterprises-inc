Invoice Factoring for B2B Businesses: Real Costs and When It Makes Sense

November 19, 2026  ·  JWAT Enterprises Inc
← Back to Blog

Invoice factoring lets you sell unpaid invoices to a third party in exchange for immediate cash — typically 70–90% of the invoice value upfront, with the remainder (minus fees) paid when your customer settles. It is not a loan. It is a way to convert work you have already done into working capital without waiting 30, 60, or 90 days.

Whether it makes sense depends almost entirely on your margin, your customer's payment habits, and what the cash gap is actually costing you.

How Invoice Factoring Works

The basic mechanics are straightforward:

  1. You complete a job and issue an invoice to your business customer (B2B or B2G only — factoring does not apply to consumer receivables).
  2. You sell that invoice to a factoring company at a discount.
  3. The factor advances you 70–90% of the face value, usually within 24–48 hours.
  4. Your customer pays the factor directly on the original due date.
  5. The factor releases the remaining balance to you, minus its fee.

The key difference from a bank line of credit is that your approval is based primarily on your customer's creditworthiness, not yours. That makes factoring accessible to younger businesses or owners with less-than-perfect credit scores.

The Real Costs of Invoice Factoring

Factoring companies quote fees in several ways, and the differences matter. Here is what to look for before you sign anything.

Flat Factor Rate

The most common structure. You pay a percentage of the invoice face value — often 1–5% — for a set period (usually 30 days). If your customer pays on day 45, some agreements charge an additional fee for each extra 15- or 30-day window. Read the renewal clause carefully.

Monthly Fee on the Advance

Some factors charge a percentage of the amount advanced rather than the invoice total. The math looks similar on paper but can compound quickly if your customer is a slow payer.

Additional Fees to Watch

  • Origination or setup fee: a one-time charge to open the account.
  • ACH or wire fee: charged each time funds are transferred to you.
  • Credit check fee: for verifying your customers' creditworthiness.
  • Minimum volume requirement: some factors require you to factor a minimum dollar amount per month or face a penalty.
  • Recourse vs. non-recourse: with recourse factoring, you are on the hook if your customer does not pay. Non-recourse protects you but costs more.
Quick math example: You factor a $20,000 invoice. The factor advances 85% ($17,000) and charges a 3% flat fee ($600). Your customer pays on day 35, triggering a small extension fee of $200. Your total cost: $800. You received $17,000 on day one and get $2,200 back when the invoice closes. Net: $19,200 on a $20,000 invoice. That is a 4% all-in cost to access cash 55 days early.

When Invoice Factoring Makes Sense

Factoring is not the right tool for every situation. It works best when specific conditions are present.

Your Customers Are Creditworthy but Slow

Large retailers, municipalities, hospitals, and construction general contractors routinely pay on 60–90 day terms. Your business may be perfectly healthy — you just cannot wait that long. Factoring converts a reliable-but-slow receivable into immediate cash.

You Have a Payroll or Supply Gap Coming

If you have a $40,000 payroll due in 10 days and $35,000 sitting in a 60-day invoice, factoring closes that gap without touching a credit line or missing payroll.

You Are Growing Faster Than Your Bank Will Finance

Banks look at your history. Factoring companies look at your customers. If you just landed a large contract but your business is only 18 months old, factoring may be the only way to fund the work without turning down the contract.

You Have Been Turned Down for a Traditional Line of Credit

Bank denials for lines of credit are common for businesses under two years old or with limited collateral. Factoring is asset-based — the invoice itself is the collateral. This makes it available to a broader range of businesses.

When Invoice Factoring Does Not Make Sense

Factoring is expensive relative to a bank line of credit. If your margins are thin — say 10–15% — giving up 3–5% of invoice value every 30 days will eat into profitability fast.

  • Consumer-facing businesses cannot use factoring. It is strictly B2B and B2G.
  • Disputed invoices will not be accepted. Factors want clean, undisputed receivables.
  • High-volume, low-margin businesses (like certain wholesale distributors) may find the fees unsustainable at scale.
  • Businesses with strong cash reserves — if you can wait and your bank offers a line at prime plus two, use the line instead.

What to Check Before You Factor an Invoice

  1. Verify your customer qualifies. The factor will run a credit check on your customer. If your customer has a weak payment history, you may be declined or offered a lower advance rate.
  2. Read the recourse clause. Know exactly what happens if your customer does not pay. Recourse factoring means the risk stays with you.
  3. Understand the notification requirement. Most factoring agreements require your customer to be notified and to redirect payment to the factor. Some business relationships are sensitive to this. Confirm before signing.
  4. Calculate the annualized cost. A 3% fee sounds small. On a 30-day invoice, that is roughly 36% annualized. Know what you are paying relative to other options.
  5. Check for long-term contracts or minimums. Some factors lock you in for 12 months or require monthly minimums. If you only need factoring occasionally, look for spot-factoring arrangements with no long-term commitment.

The Bottom Line

Invoice factoring is a legitimate, widely used tool for B2B businesses that have solid customers but cannot afford to wait on payment. The costs are real and need to be built into your pricing or margin model before you rely on it regularly.

Use it when the cash gap has a clear business cost — a missed payroll, a stalled contract, an opportunity you cannot fund — and when your customer's creditworthiness supports a reasonable advance rate. Avoid it when your margins cannot absorb the fees or when a cheaper form of credit is already available to you.

If you are carrying multiple outstanding invoices and want to explore whether a broader working capital line might serve you better than factoring each invoice individually, it is worth running the numbers on both before you commit to a structure.
