Funding for Government Contractors: Covering Payroll While You Wait to Get Paid

November 19, 2026  ·  JWAT Enterprises Inc
← Back to Blog

Government contracts pay well — but they pay slowly. If payroll is due before your invoice clears, a working-capital loan or revenue-based advance can bridge that gap. Lenders who specialize in business financing understand net-30, net-60, and even net-90 payment cycles, and some can move in as little as 24–48 hours.

Why Government Contractors Run Into Cash-Flow Problems

Winning a federal, state, or municipal contract feels like a milestone. Then the payment cycle kicks in and reality sets in fast. Government agencies are not known for paying quickly. Net-60 and net-90 terms are common. Some invoices run even longer when they hit bureaucratic slowdowns or fiscal year transitions.

Meanwhile, your obligations do not pause. Payroll runs on a fixed schedule. Subcontractors want to be paid on time. Equipment needs to be maintained or replaced. Insurance premiums come due. Every one of those costs hits your account whether or not the government has cut your check yet.

This is not a sign that your business is struggling. It is a structural feature of government contracting — and it catches even experienced contractors off guard when a project scales up faster than their cash reserves.

The Specific Costs That Stack Up Between Invoice and Payment

It helps to map out exactly where the pressure comes from, so you can size the financing you actually need.

  • Payroll: Employees expect to be paid on time, every time. Missing payroll — even once — damages trust and can trigger legal liability.
  • Payroll taxes and benefits: Federal and state payroll taxes are due on a fixed schedule regardless of your receivables.
  • Subcontractor payments: Many subs operate on thin margins themselves. Delayed payment strains the relationship and may put your project timeline at risk.
  • Equipment and materials: Some contracts require you to front materials costs before reimbursement. That gap adds up quickly on larger jobs.
  • Bonding and insurance premiums: Performance bonds and general liability coverage are non-negotiable on most government contracts. Lapses can disqualify you from future work.
  • Overhead: Rent, utilities, software, and administrative costs continue whether you are waiting on one invoice or five.
The core problem: Your revenue is real and documented. The contract exists. The work is complete. But the cash is not in your account yet — and your obligations do not care about that distinction.

Financing Options Worth Knowing

Not every funding product is built for the contractor cash-flow problem. Here is how the most common options compare.

Option How It Works Best For Watch Out For
Contract Financing / Working Capital Loan Lender advances funds against your contract value or revenue history Covering payroll and overhead while waiting on invoices Qualification requirements vary by lender
Invoice Factoring You sell your outstanding invoice to a factoring company at a discount and receive cash now Immediate liquidity on a specific invoice Fees reduce your effective payment; government invoices require an approved factor
Revenue-Based Advance Advance based on overall business revenue, repaid as a percentage of future revenue Businesses with consistent monthly revenue Factor rates vary; total repayment may be higher than a term loan
Business Line of Credit Revolving credit you draw from as needed Recurring cash-flow gaps across multiple contracts May require stronger credit profile; approval timelines vary
SBA Loan Government-backed loan with favorable terms Longer-term capital needs Application and approval can take weeks to months

For contractors facing an immediate payroll deadline, the SBA timeline alone makes it a poor fit. Working capital products that move faster are worth exploring first.

What Lenders Look at for Contractor Financing

Alternative lenders and lending marketplaces evaluate government contractors differently than traditional banks do. Here is what typically matters most.

Time in Business

Most lenders want to see at least six months to a year of operating history. The longer your track record, the more options open up.

Monthly or Annual Revenue

Lenders use your revenue history to gauge your ability to repay. Documented contracts — even if unpaid — may strengthen your application because they show future receivables.

Credit Profile

Some lenders will consider applicants with scores as low as 500. A stronger credit score expands your options and may improve your terms. If you have not checked your business credit recently, that is a good first step before applying.

Contract Documentation

Having a copy of your government contract, award letter, or outstanding invoice ready can speed up the process. It demonstrates that the revenue you are borrowing against actually exists.

Outstanding Debt

Existing loans or advances affect how much additional financing you can qualify for. Be prepared to disclose current obligations.

One application, multiple lenders: Instead of applying to lenders one at a time, a lending marketplace submits your information to multiple lenders at once. This saves time and allows lenders to compete for your business — which can mean better terms.

How to Use Financing Without Putting Your Business at Risk

Financing is a tool. Used precisely, it protects your payroll and your relationships. Used carelessly, it compounds the problem. A few principles to keep in mind:

  1. Borrow only what the gap requires. Calculate the exact shortfall — payroll plus any non-deferrable obligations — and request that amount. Borrowing more than you need increases your repayment cost.
  2. Know your repayment timeline before you sign. Map your expected invoice payment date against the repayment schedule. Confirm there is enough buffer if the government payment is delayed further.
  3. Read the full repayment terms. Factor rates and percentage-of-revenue repayments can look affordable upfront but add up over a longer term. Understand the total cost, not just the advance amount.
  4. Keep your contract documents organized. Lenders move faster when you can produce supporting documentation quickly. Award letters, invoices, and bank statements should be accessible at any time.
  5. Consider a line of credit if gaps are recurring. If your contracts consistently run net-60 or longer, a revolving line of credit may be more efficient than applying for a new advance each time a gap appears.

Acting Before Payroll Is Due — Not After

The worst time to apply for financing is 48 hours before payroll. Even fast-moving lenders need time to review your application, verify documentation, and process the transfer. Applying a week or more before the gap hits gives you room to compare offers and choose the right product.

If you have an outstanding government invoice and can see that the payment will not arrive before your next payroll run, that is the moment to start the process — not after you have already missed a paycheck.

The Bottom Line

A slow-paying government contract does not have to mean a missed payroll. The gap between completing the work and receiving payment is predictable, which means it is also plannable. Working-capital financing — applied for early and sized correctly — keeps your team paid, your subcontractors on board, and your business positioned to take on the next contract instead of scrambling to recover from this one.
